Non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis 
 
The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences has decided to award Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai the 
Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026, for discovering non-linear effects in asymmetric catalysis and 
asymmetric autocatalysis. 
Introduction 
The discovery and theoretical explanation by Henri B. Kagan of non-linear effects (NLE) in 
asymmetric catalysis, one of the most important classes of transformations in organic synthesis, has 
shown that the enantiomeric excess (ee)1 of the product can exceed that of the auxiliary used in the 
process, a situation that was previously unthinkable. The most powerful manifestation to date of NLE 
in asymmetric catalysis was discovered by Kenso Soai, and the reaction now bears his name: the 
Soai reaction. In this autocatalytic reaction, a dialkylzinc reagent is added to an aldehyde, and the 
product acts as the catalyst in the process. When starting from achiral reagents and in the absence of 
added catalysts, the product is formed in considerable ee in an absolute asymmetric transformation. 
These discoveries have deepened our understanding of chirality and how it is generated and 
transmitted, with significance far beyond chemistry. As an example, the central molecules of life – 
amino acids and sugars – are homochiral.2 They are used to build proteins and enzymes, which 
underpin structure and catalysis of a cell, and DNA and RNA, which are essential for containing and 
transferring genetic information. The uniform chirality of these building blocks is crucial for their 
function; without it, our current life forms could not exist.3 
For this homochirality to evolve requires a pool of amino acids and sugars that is enantiomerically 
pure or at least has an ee. However, abiotic synthesis of amino acids without the influence of an 
external chiral force invariably results in the formation of a racemate, i.e. an equal amount of the two 
enantiomers,4 and similar arguments apply for the synthesis of sugars. Thus, understanding how 
biological homochirality came about starting from racemic or achiral precursors is one of the most 
fundamental questions in chemistry, as well as in other areas, and central to understanding prebiotic 
chemistry that eventually resulted in the evolution of life on Earth. 
The origin of homochirality in biological systems is a conundrum that has received considerable 
attention. Today, the prevailing argument is that homochirality came about through a progressive 
process, perhaps coupled to abiotic chemical evolution, that ultimately favoured D-sugars and L-
amino acids.5-13 Such a scenario requires an initial imbalance between enantiomers, and assuming 
3 
this scenario, the question is: what chemical processes could have been responsible for such an initial 
imbalance?14 
The discussion thus leads to a fundamental question that is of general relevance to all of chemistry: 
are there any chemical mechanisms that could lead to an increase of ee or even to homochirality when 
starting from a pool of substrate that is achiral or has only a small ee? This fundamental question is 
the topic of this year’s Nobel Prize in Chemistry. 
In 1953, Sir Frederick Charles Frank, a theoretical physicist at the University of Bristol with broad 
research interests, proposed a mathematical model describing how homochirality can be generated 
from an initial minuscule imbalance between R and S enantiomers.15,16 The aim was to provide a 
model for the creation of homochirality in biological systems, but the results can be applied to all 
closed systems. From the introductory part of Frank's paper, it is clear that he judged the problem to 
be trivial, at least from a theoretical aspect. 
The presumption in the Frank model "is a chemical substance which is a catalyst for its own 
production and an anti-catalyst for the production of its optical antimer", which boils down to three 
discrete criteria for such processes: (i) autocatalysis, (ii) enantioselective catalysis, and (iii) inhibition 
("mutual antagonism"). Frank concluded his report by laconically noting that "A laboratory 
demonstration may not be impossible." 
The first of the three requirements, namely autocatalysis, is a well-known phenomenon in organic 
chemistry.17 For example, acetic acid catalyses the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate into acetic acid and 
ethanol; in other words, acetic acid catalyses its own formation from ethyl acetate. In more advanced 
settings, it has been shown that oligonucleotides18 and peptides19 can catalyse their own formation. 
The second requirement, enantioselective or asymmetric catalysis, is also well documented, and 
aspects of it were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2001 (asymmetric catalysis) and 2021 
(asymmetric organocatalysis). However, neither the third requirement, mutual antagonism, nor the 
combination of autocatalysis and asymmetric catalysis, asymmetric autocatalysis, had been described 
prior to the discoveries of Kagan and Soai. 
Mutual inhibition, or NLE in asymmetric catalysis, was described in 1986 by Kagan, who showed 
that the product from an asymmetric epoxidation had higher enantiomeric excess (eeprod) than that of 
the catalyst (eecat) used for the transformation.20-22 A mechanistic rationale for the phenomenon was 
also provided. Since then, NLE has become an important tool for understanding asymmetric catalysis 
and has been used for designing new effective asymmetric catalytic processes. Today it is a 
fundamental concept in organic chemistry that has expanded our appreciation of chirality. 
4 
In 1990, Soai showed that the addition of dialkylzinc reagents to pyridine-3-carbaldehyde in the 
presence of a chiral catalyst, the product, is both autocatalytic and enantioselective, but the product 
is obtained in lower ee compared to that of the catalyst (eeprod 35%, eecat 86%).23 In this experiment, 
two of the three criteria in the Frank model were fulfilled. 
Five years later, Soai and colleagues reported an asymmetric autocatalytic reaction in which the 
product had a higher ee than the catalyst;24, 25 i.e. all three criteria in the Frank model were fulfilled. 
A successful experiment had demonstrated how homochirality can be created in a chemical 
laboratory. 
It is important to stress that the Frank model is not an answer to the origin of biological homochirality. 
It provides one possible solution, of which there are several, to an event that occurred about 3.5–
4 billion years ago and to which we will probably never have a definitive answer. Instead, the 
experimental validation provided by Kagan and Soai to the Frank model has deepened our 
fundamental understanding of how chirality is generated, transmitted and amplified in asymmetric 
catalysis and thus of intrinsic molecular properties that are central to organic chemistry and related 
disciplines. 
Non-linear effects in asymmetric catalysis 
For NLE, the output of a system is not directly proportional to a specific variable. Interactions between 
enantiomers have long been known to result in homochiral (R–R and S–S for dimers) and heterochiral 
(R–S, for dimers) aggregates, usually with different physical properties. 
Milan Uskokovic  showed that  at a high concentration , the 1H NMR spectra of racemic and 
enantiomerically pure dihydroquinine are not identical; he and colleagues observed doubling of some 
lines in the spectra when analysing non-racemic mixtures, and they ascribed the effect to 
diastereomeric solute-solute interactions.26 Similarly, when studying the optical rotation, []D, of 2-
ethyl-2-methylsuccinic acid, Alain Horeau found a deviation from the expected linear correlation 
between ee of the measured solution and the optical rotation ,27 and several related examples have 
been described in the literature.28, 29 The non-linearity observed in these examples relates to aggregate 
formations, both homochiral and heterochiral, that have different physical properties. However, it had 
not been shown that such interactions could affect chemical reactivity. 
Hans Wynberg and Ben Feringa discussed the situation where an R-isomer reacts with a reagent to 
form a product compared to that of a racemate reacting with the same reagent  (Fig. 1a).30, 31 In the 
first case, the R-isomer is surrounded by and has non-bonding interactions with other R-isomers, 
while in the second, it interacts with both R- and S-isomers. They argued that different intermolecular 
 5 
non-bonding interactions are involved that could affect the activation energies for the two reactions, 
and thus the reaction rates, and affect the product ratios for the two cases; this was referred to as the 
antipodal interaction. 
The antipodal effect was clearly manifested in the oxidative dimerization of tetrahydronaphthalene 
derivative 1 into dimer 2 (Fig. 1b). When dimerizing a racemic sample, a clear preference was evident 
for coupling of monomers with the same configuration compared to coupling of monomers with 
different configurations (73.9% RR, SS and 26.1% RS); this is a consequence of the enantiomeric 
recognition effect, which refers to what extent an isomer recognises an identical or antipodal isomer.32 
In addition, the coupling of the S-monomer was stereospecific, affording only the (S,S)-trans product, 
while in the coupling of the racemate, the (RR,SS)-cis isomer was also obtained in 7.9%. The presence 
of the (RR,SS)-cis isomer in the latter reaction was inferred to be the influence of the R-monomer on 
the coupling reaction of the S-monomer, and an example of the antipodal interaction. From this study, 
it was clear that diastereomeric interactions not only can affect physical properties of a system , but 
also can affect chemical reactivity. The same authors also noted that antipodal effects can be of 
significance in asymmetric synthesis for cases when (reactant)n-metal complexes are formed.30

Prior to 1986 , it was an accepted fact that the enantiomeric excess of the product ( eeprod) in 
asymmetric catalytic reactions should correlate linearly with the ee of the ligand (eeligand), according 
to Eq. 1: 
 eeprod = eemax x eeligand (1) 
where eemax is the enantiomeric excess of the product when using an enantiomerically pure reagent 
(eeligand = 100%, Fig. 2a, line a). That year, the field was transformed by a study by Kagan and co-
workers that redefined chemists’ perception of chirality and catalysis.20 

 6 
They noted that the above relationship should hold true for catalysts of the type ML*, where M is a 
metal and L* a chiral ligand. To break the linear relationship between eeprod and eeligand, Kagan and 
colleagues realised that higher order species must be involved , e.g. ML* 2. For such systems , the 
situation is more complex ; it is interest ing to note that Kagan had already contemplated this 
possibility in the late 1960s.21 
The groundbreaking work described in 1986 analysed the ML* 2 system in detail. For the case when 
the ligand is not enantiomerically pure ( eeligand < 100%), in addition to the homochiral complexes 
MLRLR and MLSLS, a diastereomeric heterochiral complex MLRLS may form. If the reactivity of the 
heterochiral catalyst MLRLS is larger than that of the homochiral species, then the enantiomeric excess 
of the product (eeprod) from such reactions will be lower than expected from a linear relationship (Eq. 
1), i.e. a negative NLE33 (–NLE, Fig. 2 a, line b). Convers ely, if the reactivity of the homochiral 
catalyst complexes is higher than that of MLRLS, then the eeprod is expected to be higher than that 
predicted from Eq. 1  (+NLE, Fig. 2a, curve c) . Thus, by invoking higher order catalyst species , a 
conceptual mechanism is established by which stereochemical purity of the product is higher than 
that of the auxiliary used to create it; i.e. the stereochemistry can be amplified. 
Three asymmetric transformations, for which it was believed that more than one unit of the chiral 
ligand would be involved in the enantiodetermining  step, were initially investigated in detail: 
asymmetric oxidation of a sulfide into the corresponding sulfoxide (Ti(O -i-Pr)4/(+)-DET/H2O, t-
BuOOH, both stoichiometric and catalytic amounts of the catalyst), Sharpless epoxidation of geraniol 
(Fig. 2b), and the L -proline–catalysed Hajos-Parrish reaction. In none of the se reactions was a 
linearity observed between eeligand and eeprod. Instead, for the first and last reactions , a –NLE was 
observed, while for the Sharpless epoxidation, a +NLE was obtained; i.e. the eeprod was higher than 
expected when using scalemic34 (+)-DET (Fig. 2b).

reaction mechanisms.

In a subsequent study , Kagan and colleagues expanded the ML*2 catalyst system to include ML *3 
and ML *4 complexes, as well as the reservoir effect .35 The latter describes the situation where 
catalytically inactive ML RLS complexes are formed , which would alter the ratio between the 
remaining LR and LS ligands that are available for catalysis.36, 37 
Such a reservoir system has been described in detail in work led by Ryoji Noyori.38, 28, 39 Nobuki Oguni 
and colleagues had shown  previously that there is a strong +NLE in the asymmetric addition of 
diethylzinc to benzaldehyde catalysed by sterically constrained chiral tertiary -aminoalcohols, and 
this stimulated research.40 In a subsequent study, Noyori and co-authors found that (–)-DAIB ((–)-3-
exo-(dimethylamino)isoborneol ) is an excellent ligand for the reaction , showing a considerable  
+NLE: when eeligand is 15%, the product is obtained in eeprod 98% (Fig. 4a).38 Investigations of the 
reaction mechanism revealed that DAIB react with diethylzinc to form DAIB-ZnEt complexes, which 
dimerize to form three different dinuclear zinc complexes, two homochiral (1 and 2, Fig. 4b) and one 
heterochiral (3). However, it is the monomeric metal-ligand species (–)-4 and (+)-4 that are the active 
catalysts in the addition reaction ; the dimers are reservoirs for the monomers. The NLE is then due 

 8 
to the greater thermodynamic stability of dimer 3 compared to that of complexes 1 and 2, with the 
result that the minor enantiomer of the scalemic DAIB ligand is not available for catalysis.

Another early example of the importance of NLE for elucidating the intricate details of catalytic 
reaction mechanisms was provided by Eric N. Jacobsen  and co -workers in their studies of the 
(salen)Cr(III)-catalysed nucleophilic ring-opening of meso epoxides.41 In this reaction, a significant 
NLE was observed, and the stereochemical outcome could not be accommodated with a monomeric 
catalyst species. Instead, detailed mechanistic investigations revealed a dual activation of the 
electrophile and nucleophile by two different metal centres in the enantio-determining step. This work 
established key concepts, such as cooperative catalysis, and assisted in incorporating NLE as an 
integrated part of mechanistic investigations. 
A common denominator in the ML*2 system discussed by Kagan is that the heterochiral dimer is less 
reactive than the homochiral , in order to provide a +NLE or, as in the addition of diethylzin c to 
benzaldehyde, a non-reactive reservoir. In both cases, eeprod cannot exceed eemax, which follows from 
Eq. 2 and is also intuitively appreciated. A detailed analysis by Kagan of the ML*3 system revealed 
that if the heterochiral metal ligand complex (MLRLRLS) is much more reactive than the homochiral 
(large g values, see Eq. 2), then the eeprod can indeed exceed eemax, but only at relatively high values 
of eeligand; the phenomenon was referred to as a hyperpositive NLE.35 
Stéphane Bellemin -Laponnaz and colleagues reported the first, and as yet only, example of  
hyperpositive NLE in the asymmetric addition of dimethylzin c to benzaldehyde using (–)-NBE 
((1R,2S)-N-Benzylephedrine) as a chiral ligand and affording 1-phenylethanol (Fig. 5a).42, 43 For this 
case, however, eeprod reached a maximum (53%) at a low ee of the ligand (eeligand 5%) and then tapered 
off to reach eeprod 16% when eeligand was 100%, clearly indicating a different mechanism compared 
to the ML*3 system. When using a scalemic ligand in the reaction , a precipitate was formed, which, 
upon hydrolysis, returned the racemic ligand . Based on a detailed mechanistic investigation , it was 

 9 
proposed that both monomeric (ML*) and dimeric catalysts (ML*•ML*) are active in this reaction. 
At low eeligand, when a dimeric heterochiral (meso, ML R•MLS) complex precipitates out from the 
reaction mixture, the concentration of the ligand will be low, and the reaction will be catalysed by a 
monomeric catalyst. Conversely, as the eeligand increases, the concentration of the available ligand 
will increase, and the reaction will be catalysed by both monomeric and dimeric catalysts. In this 
reaction, the monomeric catalyst is more enantioselective than the dimeric one.

In a series of studies , Donna G. Blackmond  has expanded the mathematical models developed by 
Kagan and co-workers, showing that the reaction rate can be expressed as a function of eecat in 
asymmetric catalytic reactions,37 and that the reaction rate and eeprod may vary with conversion.44, 45 
Her work has clearly demonstrated that the analysis of NLE can be a powerful diagnostic tool for 
gaining mechanistic insight and information about the active catalytic species.44 
Blackmond and colleagues also showed that asymmetric amplification can operate in asymmetric 
reactions without invoking ML*n systems. They observed an NLE in an aldol reaction catalysed by 
L-proline in a mixed phase system. 46 They showed that when scalemic proline is used at 
concentrations above the solubility limit, a racemic compound will precipitate, and the resulting 
eutectic composition will correspond to an ee of about 50% of the proline remaining in solution. As 
a consequence, in the proline-catalysed aldol reaction investigated, a +NLE was observed at eeproline 
<50% and a (–)-NLE at higher ee. Similar observations were also reported independently by Yujiro 
Hayashi.47 In a later investigation of an organocatalytic cascade reaction, Blackmond and Jordi Burés 
suggested that the observed NLE could be explained by a disruption of pre -equilibria in reversible 
steps occurring before an irreversible product forming step, and they stressed that understanding the 
origin of NLE can be of importance when designing new such reactions.48, 49 
A fundamental aspect of the ML *2-system when adding scalemic chiral ligands is that the 
stereoisomer in excess is used to deactivate the minor stereoisomer  by forming the less reactive 
heterochiral dimers. An alternative strategy , referred to as chiral poisoning, is to use a readily 
available chiral additive to selectively deactivate one of the stereoisomers of a racemic or scalemic 
catalyst; such a strategy is particularly attractive when the enantiomerically pure catalyst is difficult 
to obtain. The concept was developed by John W. Faller, who applied it to a Rh-catalysed asymmetric 
hydrogenation reaction with racemic chiraphos50 as a chiral ligand, and using readily available (S)-

 10 
methophos51 as the chiral poison, and to a Ti-racemic BINOL -catalysed asymmetric allylation 
reaction, in which diisopropyl D-tartrate was used as the chiral poison.52, 53 Instead of deactivating a 
specific stereoisomer of a catalyst , it can be activated by an additive, and this approach was 
demonstrated by Koichi Mikami  in the Ti -racemic BINOL -catalysed glyoxalate ene reaction .54 
Similar strategies have also been used with great success by Oliver Trapp to enrich one stereoisomer 
of configurationally flexible biphenyl catalysts.55 
A striking example of the power of +NLE was provided by researchers at Merck in the synthesis of 
L-699,392, a leukotriene antagonist.56-58 A key step in this process is a reduction of a prochiral ketone 
to the corresponding secondary alcohol using stoichiometric amounts of -
chlorodiisopinocampheylborane. The reducing agent is prepared from -pinene and chloroborane-
methylsulfide complex, and when using -pinene of 70% optical purity, the product was obtained in 
95% ee. 
Since the discovery of the NLE in asymmetric catalysis , several additional examples have been 
documented, indicating the generality of the phenomenon.36 
Asymmetric autocatalysis – the Soai reaction 
In an asymmetric autocatalytic process, the chiral product serves as a catalyst for its own formation. 
For example, in a reaction in which an ( R) catalyst is used , the (R) catalyst will be preferentially 
formed (Fig. 6a), and such reactions have been considered important for the formation of biological 
homochirality on Earth.36 
However, such processes are not sufficient for attaining amplification of the (R) isomer.59, 60 If the ee 
of the catalyst (eeligand) is 100% and it generates a product with an ee of 90% (eemax), then eeprod will 
be 90%. When this product is used as a catalyst, the product will be eeprod = 81%, and this erosion of 
the ee will continue in subsequent reactions. To reach an increased ratio of the (R) stereoisomer, the 
catalyst must not only promote the formation of itself but also be "an anti-catalyst for the production 
of its optical antimer".15

In 1989, Albert Alberts and Wynberg showed that the addition of diethylzinc to benzaldehyde, 
catalysed by an orthotitanate prepared from TiCl 4 and scalemic (R)-1-phenyl-1-propanol-d1 (eeligand 
70%), yielded ( R)-1-phenyl-1-propanol with eeprod 32%, a process that was referred to as 
enantioselective autoinduction.61 In this process, the chiral non-deuterated ligand is obtained as the 
product, but not the chiral catalyst, which is a criteri on for autocatalysis. A year earlier, Oguni and 
colleagues had reported a significant +NLE in the addition of diethylzinc to benzaldehyde catalysed 
by scalemic sterically constrained , chiral tertiary aminoalcohols  (vide supra ),40 and Wynberg  
speculated that this reaction, if a suitable substrate can be identified, might be a strong candidate for 
developing an asymmetric autocatalytic process.62 
Indeed, in 1990, Soai, who had been investigating the asymmetric addition of dialkylzinc to aromatic 
and aliphatic aldehydes in the presence of chiral aminoalcohols ,63, 64 discovered that the addition of 
diisopropylzinc to pyridine -3-carbaldehyde, using a catalyst prepared from alcohol 1 (eeligand 86%) 
and i-Pr2Zn, afforded alcohol 1 (eeprod 35%) with the same absolute configuration as that used for 
preparing the catalyst ( Fig. 6b).23 A similar autocatalytic behaviour was also observed when using 
ferrocenyl aldehyde and an aromatic dialdehyde, together with their corresponding alcohols, as 
coupling partners.65, 66 An asymmetric autocatalytic process had indeed been discovered, and the next 
step was to investigate if it was possible to attain amplification of the stereochemistry, a +NLE. 
Five years later , Soai showed that alcohol ( S)-4, of only a small ee, catalyse s the addition of 
diisopropylzinc to pyrimidine-5-carbaldehyde to promote the formation of ( S)-4 with an impressive 
+NLE (Fig. 7).24 For example, when a catalyst was prepared from (S)-4 with eeligand 5% and used in 
the addition of diisopropylzin c to pyrimidine -5-carbaldehyde ( 2), alcohol ( S)-4 was obtained in 
eeprod 55% – a considerable amplification compared to the stereochemical purity of that of the original 
catalyst (Entry 1).24 When the product from this reaction, the newly formed alcohol (S)-4, as well as 

 12 
the ligand used for preparing the catalyst, was used as a catalyst for the addition of i-Pr2Zn to aldehyde 
2, the product was again obtained with an increased enantiomeric excess ( eeprod 87%, Entry 2). This 
sequence was then repeated until a plateau was reached at eeprod 90% (Entries 3–5). Thus, through a 
series of five consecutive runs, the ee of alcohol (S)-4 could be amplified from 5% to 90%. 
The autocatalytic asymmetric addition of dialkylzin c to pyrimidine -5-carbaldehyde and similar 
derivatives with amplification of the stereochemical information is now referred to as the Soai 
reaction.67-70 An even more spectacular example was identified when it was shown that addition of i-
Pr2Zn to aldehyde 3 using alkynyl derivate 5 of extremely low enantiomeric excess ( eeligand 
0.00005%) provided alcohol 5 with eeprod >99.5% after three consecutive runs (Entries 6 –8), which 
corresponds to an amplification of the stereoisomeric ratio by a factor of 630,000.71 In addition to the 
pyrimidyl derivatives shown in Fig. 7, similar amplifying asymmetric autocatalysis has been shown 
in only a few other systems.72-75

It has also been shown that racemates of organic molecules, when treated with circular polarised light, 
can induce high eeprod in the Soai reaction.76 Other instances include the chirality of minerals, organic 
crystals and surfaces,77 as well as isotope chirality78.69 
The Soai reaction is a good platform for testing the possibility of symmetry breaking: i.e. if there can 
be a prefe rential formation of one stereoisomer without any stereochemical informational input, a 
process referred to as absolute asymmetric synthesis .79 Such model systems are  of considerable 
significance when discussing the origin of biological homochirality.80 Such symmetry breaking had 
previously only been documented in spontaneous deracemization processes.81 

 13 
Both Soai and Daniel Singleton, independently of each other, showed that this is indeed possible with 
the Soai reaction (Fig. 8, R = t-BuCC in Soai’s experiment,82 and R = Me in Singleton’s83, 84).85 When 
diisopropylzinc is added to aldehyde 3, the zin c alkoxide 6 is formed as a racemate. In such a 
molecular aggregate that is macroscopically characteri sed as an equal mixture of the two 
stereoisomers, however, there will be a statistical fluctuation over time that might favour a stochastic 
miniscule preference for either of the two stereoisomers , a phenomenon referred to by Kurt Mislow 
as cryptochirality .86 The discriminating power, the +NLE, of the Soai reaction is then powerful 
enough to sense the imbalance between the stereoisomers of 6, which will be manifested in the 
stereochemical outcome from the reaction. Indeed, during a series of 37 experiments, 18 resulted in 
a preferential formation of (R)-6, and 19 gave (S)-6, the eeprod varying from 15% to 91%.82 
The discovery of the Soai reaction was also the first successful laboratory experiment to verify the 
Frank model for generation of biological homochirality in the absence of external chiral influence. It 
is indeed a significant accomplishment , showing that enantiomerically enriched or homochiral 
products can be generated from non-chiral starting materials. The Soai reaction is an important proof 
of concept , though it  is not relevant f or the emergence of biological homochirality in aqueous 
systems. Contemporary efforts to resolve this issue instead focus on network processes and kinetic 
resolution.7, 87

Developing an understanding of reaction mechanisms is a fundamental part of organic chemistry, and 
the rationale that emerges from such activities is used for prediction, control and, perhaps most 
intellectually rewarding, design of new and as-yet untested reactions that can address currently 
unattainable problems. The Soai reaction is the only example so far of an asymmetric autocatalytic 
reaction with amplification of stereochemistry and that is also applicable to absolute asymmetric 
synthesis. 

 14 
Erick Carreira and colleagues have developed an asymmetric autocatalytic process for the efficient 
synthesis of a key intermediate used for the preparation of Efavirenz, an antiretroviral drug used for 
the treatment of HIV-infection.88 For this case, however, no details of NLE were reported. 
For the past three decades, the elucidation of a detailed reaction mechanism  for NLE has been a 
challenging quest that has received considerable interest and has involved several research groups . 
These efforts have been summarised in an excellent review.67 Here, only a brief summary is outlined 
of the timeline leading up to our current understanding of the reaction mechanism of the Soai reaction. 
In the original work led by Soai from 1990, which included discussion of autocatalysis with erosion 
of the stereochemical information , the reaction was proposed to involve a catalytically active zinc 
monoalkoxide.23 Five years later, the asymmetric autocatalysis with amplification of the 
stereochemistry was described; however , it was concluded that detailed understanding of the 
mechanism remained to be clarified, particularly the underlying reason for the +NLE.24 
After half a decade, Blackmond, John Brown and collaborators investigated the reaction heat in the 
Soai reaction and concluded that the data were most consistent with a dimeric catalyst ; i.e. the 
mechanism previously proposed by Noyori for the addition of dialkylzinc to aldehydes is not 
applicable in that case.89 In a kinetic modelling of the reaction, Blackmond provided evidence for a 
tetrameric transition state and reported that the reaction rate and +NLE could be accurately predicted 
over a wide range of reaction conditions.90 
In 2007, based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations, Jürgen Klankermayer, Ilya Gridnev 
and Brown suggested a square -macrocycle-square (SMS) as a most likely end product in a 
mechanistic pathway , thus superseding the previous dimeric model. 91 Observation of an unusual 
inverse temperature dependence on the reaction rate in the Soai reaction, together with kinetic studies 
and NMR investigation, led Brown and Blackmond to propose in 2010 that the active catalyst is 
derived from the SMS tetrameric species .92 Two years later , Gridnev proposed a catalytic cycle 
involving the SMS zinc alkoxide, based on DFT calculations.93 
In 2015, by mixing diisopropyllzinc and 2-alkynylpyrimidylalkanols, Arimasa Matsumoto, Tsuneomi 
Kawasaki and Soai were able to isolate crystals of the alkoxides, the structures of which were 
determined by X-ray crystallography: when using an excess of i-Pr2Zn, homochiral and heterochiral 
tetramers were obtained, the structures of which resemble the SMS complex. In these structures, two 
zinc alkoxides form Zn2O2 square dimers, and two of these coordinate with each other via an aromatic 
nitrogen atom of one dimer and a zinc atom of the other dimer, resulting in a 12 -membered 
macrocycle. Both tetramers contain two unsaturated zinc atoms with the potential to function as 
 15 
Lewis acids and activate an aldehyde, which is required for catalysis. 94 When instead using 1 -2 
equivalents Et2Zn, oligomeric structures were obtained. 
The discussion above is complicated by the fact that different mechanisms have been proposed for 
different substrates (pyridyl -, pyrimidyl -, and 2 -alkynylpyrimidyl carbaldehydes) and different 
reagents (Et2Zn and i-Pr2Zn). Further weaknesses are that the mechanisms do not explain (i) how the 
SMS complex, if it is the active catalyst, functions and binds the substrate, (ii) the enantioselectivity 
and +NLE, (iii) the narrow substrate scope of the Soai reaction, and (iv) the cause of the inverse 
temperature dependence. 
Responding to this challenge, Scott Denmark and colleagues proposed a detailed mechanism for the 
Soai reaction .95-97 Based on kinetic studies, including surrogate substrates, spectroscopic 
investigations of zinc alkoxides, and DFT calculations, they proposed a mechanism involving the 
Soai SMS complex in which the substrate coordinates to the homochiral tetramer catalyst through a 
floor-to-floor arrangement involving two coordinatively unsaturated zinc atoms. In this scenario, 
transfer of an alkyl moiety to the activated aldehyde then delivers the correct stereoisomeric product. 
Such floor -to-floor coordination of the substrate is, for geometrical reasons, disfavoured in the 
heterochiral tetramer, which was clearly indicated in the DFT calculation. This then accounts for the 
amplification of stereochemistry  in the autocatalytic reaction  and is in line with both the Kagan 
principle for asymmetric amplification and the Frank model for generation of homochirality. 
At about the same time, Oliver Trapp and colleagues investigated the Soai reaction mechanism using 
in situ  HRMS spectrosc opic methods, kinetic simulations and DFT calculations , and proposed a 
mechanism involving transient hemiacetal zinc complexes  (product-substrate dimer) .98-100 In this 
mechanism, a diisopropyl zinc alkoxide monomer is in equilibrium with its homo - and heterochiral 
dimers, with the homochiral structure being the most favoured thermodynamically. Reaction of the 
monomer with the substrate aldehyde forms a zinc hemiacetal complex and is the slowest step in the 
catalytic cycle. Addition of the substrate and i-Pr2Zn to this intermediate, followed by intramolecular 
alkyl transfer and coordination of an additional substrate aldehyde, results in a complex that can now 
be cleaved into two identical zinc hemiacetal catalysts. 
In this model , the asymmetric amplification is due to the slow and unfavourable formation of the 
hemiacetal, but once it is formed, the much faster autocatalytic reaction can proceed. The homo- and 
heterochiral dimers are not responsible for entrapment of the minor enantiomer and instead provide 
a kinetic barrier between the monomers and the catalytic cycle. Here, the asymmetric amplification 
is due to a kinetic ally controlled nonequilibrium stationary state, which is different from the 
 16 
mechanism put forward by Kagan and Frank. The insights gained from this investigation can provide 
an impetus for developing new asymmetric autocatalytic processes, as argued by the authors. 
It can be noted that  zinc acetals had previously be en detected as intermediates in the Soai reaction 
using 1H NMR spectroscopy.101 However, they were later judged not to be involved in the catalytic 
cycle based on results from DFT calculations.102  
In addition to this, Jean-Claude Micheau and Thomas Buhse have used XRD data, DFT calculations 
and selected experimental kinetic data  to build a kinetic and thermodynamic model of the Soai 
reaction.103 
Two detailed mechanisms have been put forward for the Soai reaction, differing among other things 
in the structure of the active catalyst , the rate determining steps  and the cause of the asymmetric 
amplification. Both models have been argued to be accommodated , considering that different 
substrates were used in the investigations by Denmark and Trapp, respectively.104 
Consequences and applications 
Chirality is a fundamental aspect of molecular structure. The discoveries by Kagan and Soai are 
groundbreaking and have reshaped our understanding of how chirality can be generated and amplified 
in a closed system. The concepts are now integrated with our current understanding of asymmetric 
catalytic processes. NLE in asymmetric catalysis offer a tool for determining reaction mechanisms 
but have also opened new perspectives in the search for alternative amplification mechanisms. The 
most illustrious example of this effect is the asymmetric autocatalysis – the Soai reaction – which 
results in homochirality without assistance of an external chiral factor. The discovery of the Soai 
reaction has vitalised the search for the origin of biological homochirality. 
The discoveries awarded with the 2026 Nobel Prize in chemistry have deepened our appreciation of 
molecular chirality, with implications that extend well beyond the realms of organic chemistry.
